artatopics

Can the positions of the planets forecast the rise and fall of research fields?

Almost certainly not in any mystical sense — which is exactly why it makes a good test case for honest forecasting. We built models whose only input is where the planets are (something known centuries in advance), tested them the strictest way we know, and published everything — especially the failures. This page is the whole story in plain words. The code and data behind every number are public at github.com/ArtaQuest/artatopics, and the live atlas is at artaquest.com/topics.

How we keep the test honest

1 — Hide the future. We fit each model using data only up to 1995, then ask it to forecast 1996–2025 and compare with what actually happened. The model never sees the years it is graded on.

2 — Always compare with doing nothing. Two lazy strategies set the bar: assume a field stays at its long-run average, or assume tomorrow looks exactly like today. A model that cannot beat those is not a model, so every score here is shown next to them.

3 — Publish the failures. Most of what we tried did not work. It is all below, and in the repository, because a search that only reports its wins cannot be trusted.

Finding 1 — one planet-tuned dial per field tracks thirty years surprisingly well

Each of 251 research fields gets its own tiny receiver: nine numbers that say how strongly it "listens" to each of seven slow planets, and one dial for when it peaks. Scores run from 1.0 (a perfect forecast) down to 0 (no better than guessing the long-run average).

Beating "nothing changes" over thirty unseen years — including the whole internet era — is a real result. It is also a bounded one: the planets here work as a set of very slow, perfectly predictable pendulums, and the model uses them as scaffolding for each field's own long rhythm. We make no claim about why it works, and none that the planets cause anything.

Finding 2 — predicting the whole pie of science: copying yesterday still wins

Different question: on a given date, how is the world's scientific attention divided among all 251 fields — which slice of the pie does each field get? We tried many planet-driven models of the whole pie at once. The winner, every time, was the simplest strategy there is: copy yesterday's pie.

strategyerror in the guessed pie ↓
copy yesterday's pie0.083
our per-field model, rescaled into a pie0.085
the best combined planet model0.088
a textbook statistical model (softmax)0.110

Smaller is better: roughly, how far the guessed pie is from the real one.

And we can say why the planet models lose: science drifts. Artificial intelligence keeps growing; older fields slowly shrink. Copying yesterday captures that drift for free. Planets, which just go round and round, have no way to encode a one-way trend — and we proved the problem was not our training method by checking every alternative: a different loss, a different model shape, more or fewer dials, restricting to only the oldest fields. Nothing changed the ranking.

Finding 3 — "will my field trend next year?" scores 68% … and the fine print is the story

For each field separately we asked a yes/no question: will next year's publication count jump by more than is typical for this field? By construction a coin flip scores 50%. Our predictors score well above that:

But here is what they actually learned — the time, not the stars. We ran the check that matters: give the predictors only the fast planets (Mars and Jupiter, which loop round many times and so carry no information about which century it is) and accuracy collapses to a coin flip. Give them only the slowest planets — which crawl so slowly they work like the hands of a clock — and all the accuracy comes back. Give them nothing but the plain calendar year, and they do better than with the sky.

In other words: publishing sped up in the modern era, "trending" years cluster in the modern era, and the slow planets simply tell the model what era it is. The predictor works — but it is reading a clock, not the stars.

The decisive picture: fast planets = coin flip, slow planets = full accuracy, a plain year number = best of all. That pattern has one honest reading: the model is telling the time.

What we claim — and what we don't

We claim exactly this: under tests where the future is hidden, the numbers above are what these models score, against the stated do-nothing baselines. We do not claim the planets cause anything, and where a model looked impressive, the controls above say plainly what it was really doing. That, more than any single score, is the point of the project.
