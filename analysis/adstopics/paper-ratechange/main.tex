\begin{abstract}
In plain words: for each of three thousand everyday topics we watch how the world's monthly YouTube
searches \emph{change} --- not how high they sit, but whether next month goes up or down --- and we
ask, over two hidden years, can that be predicted? A companion study asked the easier question (is
next month high or low against a fixed bar) and scored $\levelAUC$ --- but almost all of that was
just \emph{levels barely move month to month}. Here we ask the honest, hard question about the
\emph{change}, and the answer is humbling: nothing we build clears about $0.60$, a coin scores
$0.50$, and the ceiling any model of this kind could reach is $\ceilOthree$.

Formally, we take the monthly rate of change $r_{i,t}=(y_{i,t}-y_{i,t-1})/\max(y_{i,t-1},1)$ of each
topic's worldwide search interest and classify its \emph{direction}. A month with no change at all
is a fat atom ($\flatPct\%$ of all month-pairs) --- so no threshold can balance the raw series; we
therefore exclude the flat months as \emph{ties}, and among the months that moved the two classes are
balanced (test $\testHigh\%$ higher), coin $=0.5$. The protocol is walls-first (train / 24-month
validation for selection only / an untouched 24-month autoregressive test), and two oracle bounds are
measured before any model runs: a per-(topic,\,month) majority oracle caps prediction at $\ceilOthree$,
and cross-year label agreement --- the series-only frontier --- is $\ceilOtwo$.

We reconsidered the model space from scratch in a tournament of seven families, adversarially verified
against holdout leakage. The result is a clean, humbling ladder (tie-excluded horizon-AUC):
persistence $\accPersist$ --- \emph{below the coin}, because month-to-month direction \textbf{mean-reverts};
base rate $\accBase$; memory-majority $\accMajority$; a phase encoder--decoder $\accEncPure$/$\accEncMem$
(it \emph{loses} here); a pooled logistic $\accPooled$; seasonal-naive $\accSnaive$; a direct
multi-horizon model $\accDirect$; monthly climatology $\accClim$; and the champion, a \textbf{refined
seasonal climatology} of the direction, $\accChampion$ (95\% CI $[\champLo,\champHi]$). The champion
genuinely beats simple memory (disjoint bootstrap intervals) but only \emph{reaches} the cross-year
frontier $\ceilOtwo$; a cross-section k-NN that borrows across topics ties it at $\accKNN$, and every
nonlinear or momentum model we tried either ties memory or overfits. The honest headline: the
direction of the rate of change in search is \textbf{memory- and seasonality-bound} --- seasonality
carries what little is predictable (persistence is worse than a coin), and the $\accChampion\!\to\!\ceilOthree$
gap is the room for signal that lives \emph{outside} the series. Every number regenerates from the
registered dataset; the champion's predictions are live on the platform's topic pages and as the
reference of a fully public challenge.
\end{abstract}

\section{Why the change, not the level}

A companion study\,\footnote{``High or low'', Journal of Seasonality, Zenodo
\texttt{10.5281/zenodo.21441511}.} asked whether next month's search interest would sit high or low
against one global bar, and reported $\levelAUC$ accuracy against a $0.9635$ ceiling. That number is
real but not deep: interest \emph{levels} are strongly persistent, so ``high or low'' is answered
almost entirely by carrying this month's state forward. The scientifically honest question is about
the \emph{change}: given how a topic's search has been moving, will next month move
\textbf{significantly higher} or not? This paper asks exactly that, on the same open vocabulary.

The target is the monthly rate of change --- shift, subtract, divide:
\begin{equation}
r_{i,t} \;=\; \frac{y_{i,t}-y_{i,t-1}}{\max(y_{i,t-1},\,1)},
\qquad
L_{i,t} \;=\; \operatorname{sign}(r_{i,t}) \;\in\; \{+1,\,-1,\,0\},
\label{eq:label}
\end{equation}
where the denominator is floored at $1$ (Trends values are integers on $0$--$100$). The label is the
\emph{direction} of that change. Two facts shape the whole study. First, $r_{i,t}=0$ exactly whenever a
month is unchanged, and that happens for $\flatPct\%$ of all month-pairs --- a fat atom that no scalar
threshold can split, so the mean of $r$ (dragged to $+0.66$ by tiny-denominator spikes) leaves a
degenerate $96/4$ set and even the median ($0$) leaves $32/68$. We therefore treat the flat months as
\textbf{ties} (the journal's accepted tie-handling): they enter no fit, no selection, no score and no
ceiling. Among the $\scoredCells$ months that actually moved, the classes are balanced --- test window
$\testHigh\%$ higher --- and a coin scores $0.5$. Second, and unlike the level task, the change is
close to a martingale: this is a genuinely hard forecasting problem, and we report it as one.

\section{Protocol and ceilings}

The vocabulary is the complete advertising taxonomy Google publishes~\cite{googleads2024verticals},
split into lowercase topics; after the audit gates \nTopics{} remain, each a \nMonths{}-month worldwide
monthly series over \winStart{}--\winEnd{}. The walls are walls-first: everything before the last 48
months trains; months $-48..-25$ are validation (selection and early-stopping only); the final 24
months are the test window, touched once, and every prediction is \emph{autoregressive} --- the model
feeds on its own calls, never the answers. Ties are excluded per month, so the scored test set is the
$\scoredCells$ moved cells; the $\tieCells$ flat cells are set aside.

Two bounds are measured before any model runs (Fig.~\ref{fig:acc}). The per-(topic,\,month) majority
oracle --- knowing which direction each topic takes most often in each calendar month across the two
test years --- caps every in-protocol predictor at $\mathrm{O_3}=\ceilOthree$. Cross-year agreement,
the fraction of moved cells whose direction repeats one year later, is $\mathrm{O_2}=\ceilOtwo$: this
is the \emph{series-only frontier}, what perfect exploitation of a topic's own yearly rhythm would
score. The gap between them ($\ceilOtwo$ to $\ceilOthree$) is precisely the headroom that requires
information beyond the series itself.

\section{The tournament: reconsidering the model space}

Because the champion of the level task (a per-topic selector) has no reason to transfer, we
reconsidered the model space from scratch: seven families, each fit and selected on validation only,
then scored once on the untouched test window, and the top candidates adversarially audited for
holdout leakage (a post-wall label scramble must leave a model's test predictions unchanged; the
champion passes bit-identically). Table~\ref{tab:board} is the full ladder.

Three findings stand out. \textbf{(1) Persistence is below the coin} ($\accPersist$): carrying this
month's direction forward is \emph{worse} than guessing, because monthly search direction
mean-reverts~\cite{gardner1985damped} --- a rise is more often followed by a fall than another rise.
Every model that beats the coin does so through \emph{seasonality}, not momentum. \textbf{(2) The
phase encoder--decoder loses} ($\accEncPure$/$\accEncMem$), and the per-topic selector that won the
level task scores only $\accSelector$: sophistication does not transfer to a near-martingale target.
\textbf{(3) The champion is the simplest honest refinement of memory}: a per-(topic, month-of-year)
climatology of the direction, recency-weighted (decay $0.8$), shrunk toward the topic base rate
(pseudocount $k=1$), with a seasonality-strength gate that falls weakly-seasonal topics back to the
global monthly pattern; all three knobs selected on validation. It scores $\accChampion$ (95\% CI
$[\champLo,\champHi]$), genuinely clearing the simple-climatology reference $\accClim$ with disjoint
bootstrap intervals, and its per-topic peak-growth months (Fig.~\ref{fig:dist}) recover the obvious
calendar --- most topics rise fastest in January.

\section{What the ceiling says}

The champion \emph{reaches} the cross-year frontier ($\accChampion$ vs $\mathrm{O_2}=\ceilOtwo$) but
does not exceed it, and sits far below the $\mathrm{O_3}=\ceilOthree$ oracle. A cross-section k-NN
that borrows direction from correlated topics ($\accKNN$) is the only model that measurably beats
within-topic climatology, and it too merely ties the frontier. The reading is unambiguous and worth
stating plainly: \textbf{the direction of the monthly rate of change in search is memory- and
seasonality-bound}. A topic's own history --- chiefly its seasonal calendar --- is essentially all the
signal a series-only model can extract, and that signal tops out around $0.60$. The $0.23$ of headroom
to the oracle ceiling is real but lives \emph{outside} the series: to predict whether search will jump
next month beyond what its own seasonal rhythm implies, a model needs exogenous information (events,
prices, releases, cross-domain demand) the series does not carry. This is the honest frontier the
level task's inflated $\levelAUC$ concealed, and recovering it is this paper's contribution.

\section{Relation to the level study}

The two studies bracket what interior models can know about this vocabulary. The level task
(``high or low'') is nearly deterministic ($\levelAUC$) because levels persist; the change task (this
paper) is nearly a martingale ($\accChampion$, coin $0.5$, frontier $\ceilOtwo$) because directions
mean-revert and only the seasonal calendar carries signal. Neither number is the ``accuracy of
predicting search''; together they say that \emph{where} search sits is easy and \emph{which way it
moves next} is hard, and that closing the change gap needs data beyond the series.
